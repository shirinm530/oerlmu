Monopoly

Market Structure

A monopolist is the sole seller in its market, so it faces the entire downward-sloping demand curve rather than a single going price. Because selling one more unit means lowering the price on every unit sold, marginal revenue falls below demand — and twice as fast. Profit is maximized where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.

Try it: Raise the demand intercept and watch Qm and Pm climb together as the whole market expands. Flatten the demand slope to make demand more elastic and see the markup over marginal cost shrink — or steepen it and watch price pull further away from cost as the deadweight-loss triangle grows. Push marginal cost up to squeeze profit from both sides, or pull it down and watch the monopoly quantity approach the efficient one. Toggle the welfare areas to separate the surplus the firm captures from the surplus that simply disappears.

Monopoly

Firm Behavior

Finding the profit-maximizing quantity is only half the story — whether the monopolist actually earns a profit or suffers a loss depends on how price compares to average total cost (ATC) at that quantity. ATC blends fixed and variable costs into a per-unit figure, while average variable cost (AVC) reflects only the cost of production itself. Because fixed costs are sunk in the short run, they shift ATC up or down but never change the quantity that maximizes profit — they only determine whether producing at that quantity is worth it, or whether the firm is better off shutting down and losing just the fixed cost.

Try it: Raise fixed costs and watch ATC lift away from AVC — profit shrinks or turns into a loss even though Qm never moves. Lower fixed costs instead and see the profit rectangle grow back. Nudge fixed costs until price lands exactly on ATC to find the break-even point, then raise marginal cost until price dips below AVC and the firm shuts down, producing nothing at all. Toggle AVC on and off to see the line that separates "operate at a loss" from "shut down."
